Why AI Training Projects Dry Up ("No Tasks," Empty Queue) and How to Keep Your Income Steady

Four well-known AI-training platforms (Outlier, DataAnnotation, Handshake AI and Prolific) show zero open roles on our board today. Meanwhile, Meridial has 547 and Lilt has 398. If your only platform goes quiet, your income goes to zero overnight. That's the most common complaint we hear from AI trainers, and it's a structural feature of the work, not bad luck.

This guide explains why queues empty, how to read the warning signs, and a simple 2–3 platform setup that keeps money coming in.

Why the queue goes empty

AI-training platforms are middlemen. An AI lab buys a batch of data ("10,000 ranked medical conversations by March"), and the platform recruits and manages people to produce it. When the batch is done, the work stops. Common causes:

  1. Project completion. The client's batch is full. This is the most common reason, and it's normal.
  2. Quality reshuffles. Platforms re-rank workers by quality score. If you fall below a threshold, you get fewer or no tasks without being formally removed.
  3. Client switches vendor. Labs move spend between platforms. When a big client leaves, whole project families vanish at once.
  4. Country or language rebalancing. The client wants more raters in a different region.
  5. Model generation changes. A new model needs different data, such as more coding, more agentic tasks or more multimodal work, and older task types disappear.
  6. Over-recruitment. Platforms often onboard more people than they need, so tasks get claimed fast.

Warning signs your platform is drying up

  • Tasks disappear from your dashboard within minutes of appearing.
  • Your project moves to "paused" or "maintenance."
  • Hourly-paid projects switch to per-task pay.
  • Reviewer feedback suddenly gets stricter.
  • Community forums fill with "EQ" (empty queue) posts.
  • The platform's public job count drops. You can watch this on the platforms page, which re-checks every listing every 24 hours.

The 2–3 platform setup

The goal is to have at least one platform producing income at any given time. A practical structure:

1. One "anchor" platform in your strongest skill

This is where your best pay comes from. Expert work (law $115/hr median, medicine $90/hr, coding $65/hr) usually sits here. Examples include Mercor, micro1 or 10x Team, depending on your field.

2. One "steady" platform with lots of roles

Pick a platform with a high open-role count and broad eligibility. That might be Meridial (547 roles) or, if you speak another language, Lilt, Welo Global or RWS TrainAI. Pay is often lower (language work has a $37/hr median), but volume helps cover the gaps.

3. One "instant" option

Something you can start without a long application, such as Mindrift's all-access tasks, or paid research studies when they're open. It won't pay much, but it's there when nothing else is.

Rules that make it work

  • Stay active on all three, even at a few hours a week, because some platforms deprioritise dormant accounts.
  • Never break exclusivity or confidentiality clauses. Read each contract.
  • Track your hours and earnings per platform in a spreadsheet. You'll see which one is actually paying.
  • Don't sit at 100% on one project. If one project is 80% of your income, start applying elsewhere now.

What to do the day your queue goes empty

  1. Don't panic-quit. Many queues refill within days or weeks.
  2. Check your quality metrics if the platform shows them. A dip might be the real cause.
  3. Ask support, politely and once, whether your project is paused or whether you've been offboarded.
  4. Apply to 3–5 fresh postings on other platforms the same day. Use job alerts so you hear about them first.
  5. Re-take any qualification tests you skipped earlier, since new projects often gate on them.
  6. Update your profile with new skills. Agentic, coding and multimodal tasks are where demand is shifting.

Budgeting for lumpy income

AI-training income is contractor income: irregular, with no sick pay and no notice period. Three habits help:

  • Keep 2–3 months of expenses in reserve before you rely on AI training as your main income.
  • Set aside tax from every payout. In the US, 25–30% is a common rule of thumb for self-employment plus income tax. Our tax guide covers the US, UK and India: this guide.
  • Count only the trailing 3-month average as "real" income, not your best week.

A real-world example

Picture a Spanish-speaking generalist working 15 hours a week on one platform at around $30/hr, earning about $450 a week. Then the project ends. With the 2–3 platform setup, the same person might already be in a Meridial language pool (median $65/hr for language roles, though roles there are mostly evergreen), have Mindrift all-access tasks as a fallback, and have an alert running for fresh Mercor generalist studies ($30–35/hr). The week the main project ends, income drops instead of disappearing.

What not to do

  • Don't create duplicate accounts to get more tasks. It's the fastest route to a permanent ban.
  • Don't pay for "priority access" or task-unlocking services. Those are scams.
  • Don't rush tasks to make up for lost hours. Quality scores fall, and fewer tasks follow.

Honest caveats

  • Some workers do get permanently offboarded without an explanation. Diversifying protects you from that too.
  • More platforms means more onboarding, unpaid assessments and admin. Three is usually the sweet spot, and more than five gets hard to manage.
  • Job counts are a signal, not a guarantee. A platform with 0 public roles may still have work for existing workers.
